# PRX Quantum Popular Summary — R1

How can one part of a closed quantum system act as a clock for another part if the system as a whole does not change with time? Relational descriptions of quantum time show that this is possible: a clock and a signal can evolve relative to each other while their total energy remains fixed. What has been less clear is what physical resource limits the amount of timing information such a stationary system can carry.

We find that there are two distinct answers, depending on where the encoded state lies in quantum state space. Away from the boundary, useful high-frequency timing information must be supported by population already present in the energy levels connected by the clock--signal exchange. At a boundary, a smooth encoding can instead create previously absent energy-level population as the signal is varied. In that case the relevant cost appears one order later, through the rate at which this population is synthesized.

We derive measurement-independent bounds for both regimes, including collective measurements on multiple copies, and show that the constants can be attained by finite systems whose total energy is exactly fixed. We also obtain a sharp sum rule when several temporal frequencies are encoded at once.

The result identifies physical constraints on purely relational timing information that are invisible to global time-translation asymmetry and to ordinary signed changes in mean energy.
